One pipeline,
two worlds

datalake’s orchestration is split into a deterministic Dagster asset graph and a standalone enrichment worker. They meet at a single handoff seam — and nearly every operational pain we have logged lives on that seam.

Refactor-research note Grounded in ADR-0002 / ADR-0003 and issues #5, #20, #22, #23, #24
  World A — Dagster asset graph: deterministic, replayable   World B — enrichment worker: stochastic, network-bound

Dagster in five minutes (a primer)

If you come from conventional ETL and a SQL warehouse, the first thing to unlearn is the unit you orchestrate. Airflow-style tools orchestrate steps — scripts and tasks with arrows between them, where a "run" is the whole chain, top to bottom. Dagster orchestrates data: the tables, files, and models themselves. You declare the data, how each piece depends on the others, and how to produce it — and the running falls out of that graph.

Once you hold that, four words carry most of the meaning you will meet in this piece:

Asset — a persistent piece of data (a DuckDB table like silver_ig_posts, a Parquet file, a view) plus the code that produces it. Materialize — run that asset’s code to (re)write it; Dagster records a timestamp per materialization, which is what "fresh" and "stale" mean. Upstream / downstream — the dependency edges of the graph: an asset reads its upstream and feeds its downstream. AssetSpec / external asset — a table that Dagster knows about and tracks but does not write itself (in this piece, gold_analyses, written by the enrichment worker).

The assumptions you inherit by reading "asset graph"

When the rest of this piece talks about the asset graph, it is assuming you accept four consequences of the model:

Ordering comes from dependencies, not a scripted sequence. The graph is data-flow edges; a "pipeline" is just a named selection of assets to materialize together (a job).

Defining an asset is not running it. Automation (or a person) decides when. This is why an asset graph can exist while nothing in it is actually advancing.

Freshness is per asset, and stale is a first-class idea. Each table carries its own last-materialization time and can carry a freshness policy ("no older than N hours").

Pure compute is the comfort zone; external calls are deliberate. A deterministic, replayable transform is what Dagster treats as normal. Network I/O (an API like Gemini) must be placed on purpose — in a resource, an ingestion producer, or an external worker — or you break replayability. That rule is exactly our ADR-0003, and it is the load-bearing reason enrichment lives outside the graph.

How data flows

datalake is a medallion lakehouse: bronze to silver to labels to gold to serving. Everything inside the first three stages is a Dagster asset — deterministic, pure, cheap to replay. Then the pipeline leaves Dagster for one stage, and comes back.

It starts with a person. A bronze scrape runs an Apify actor, gated by a ScrapeConfig the user supplies in the launchpad: a list of profile URLs, a results type, a charge cap. At scrape time, media bytes are downloaded into media_cache, because Instagram CDN URLs expire in roughly four to five days. From there the graph takes over: silver (ig_posts_slv) deduplicates and normalizes bronze in DuckDB as a pure transform; the labels stage applies Tukey-fence classification to mark each post standout, control, or floor_filler within its profile cohort; and the batch asset enqueues enrichment candidates as rows in ops.sqlite.

The end of the pipeline is the serving layer: dim_profile and dim_date dimensions plus roughly seventeen analytics views such as v_post_metrics, consumed by the React dashboard as a thin projector. Nothing in serving knows or cares who produced gold.

bronze · ig_posts_raw

Manual Apify actor run, config-driven URL list. Media bytes cached at scrape time.

silver · ig_posts_slv

Pure DuckDB transform: dedup + normalize. No network I/O, by rule (ADR-0003).

labels · ig_post_labels

Tukey-fence classification per profile cohort: standout, control, floor_filler.

enrichment · outside Dagster

Standalone one-shot CLI: claims queue rows, calls Gemini, writes gold_analyses, reports materialization back over REST.

serving · dims + views

dim_profile, dim_date, ~17 analytics views. The dashboard is a thin projector over these.

Where the two worlds meet

World A never calls the network. World B is nothing but the network. The only things they share are two tables in ops.sqlite and one asset key.

World A is the Dagster asset graph. Bronze, silver, and labels are pure functions over files and DuckDB tables: replay any of them and you get the same result. It is also entirely manual. There are no sensors, no Dagster Sources, and the only two schedules that exist — daily_medallion and core_refresh — are both DefaultScheduleStatus.STOPPED. Scrapes are driven by a config URL list, not by the profile roster.

World B is scripts/enrichment_worker.py, a standalone one-shot CLI. It reads the ops.sqlite batch queue, calls Gemini (interactive generate_content, File-API media upload, or an async gemini-batch submit), writes gold_analyses into DuckDB, and reports the materialization back to Dagster over REST — a POST that currently lands on a 308 redirect. That redirect is non-fatal noise, but it is also the whole relationship: an out-of-band event, not a run.

The seam, precisely: the batch queue plus gold_analyses-as-AssetSpec. Dagster enqueues candidates in World A; the worker consumes them and writes gold in World B; Dagster then sees gold only as an externally-materialized AssetSpec carrying a FreshnessPolicy (warn at 24 hours, fail at 48). Lineage across the seam is eventual, not run-derived — the direct, accepted consequence of ADR-0002.

Enrichment is the only stage in the pipeline that is non-deterministic, rate-limited, and paid — and it is the one stage that lives outside the orchestrator.the systemic consequence of ADR-0002 + ADR-0003

The overnight stall: a loop only humans advance

Issue #24 made the split visible. A gemini-batch job — 593 posts from the 50 freshly discovered accounts — sat at 543 items “processing” for about twelve hours overnight while gold stayed flat. Nothing was wrong: the batch had finished on Google’s side. But the worker is a one-shot CLI that does one submit-then-poll cycle and exits. When its bounded loop stopped at ~00:05 with the batch still running, nothing polled again. Gold only grew the next morning, 9,035 to 9,570, because someone re-ran the worker by hand.

This is the orchestration vacuum in its purest form. Two schedules exist (daily_medallion, core_refresh) and both are STOPPED by design — enablement is a decision gate, not an oversight. But with no sensors and no running schedule, every progression step in the pipeline is a manual command: the scrape, the medallion pass, and above all the enrichment consume loop. The research note’s synthesis is blunt: the queue-with-dead-letter design is right; the missing piece is making the consume loop a sensor citizen — polling “is there work?” and “is the remote batch done?” — which ADR-0002 never precluded.

Issue #24 · diagnosed 2026-09-06

An async Gemini batch sat finished on Google’s side for ~12 hours

543 items in processing, gold flat. One manual enrichment_worker.py --mode gemini-batch re-run retrieved 535 + failed 8. Not a bug: nothing was harvesting. Root cause: no persistent or scheduled enrichment-consume loop, and no alert on “no worker polling for N hours.”

Where things break: the network boundary

Every arrow that crosses a process boundary touches the network, and each crossing has its own failure mode. The annotated flow below pins them to the exact stage where they bite.

The 429 taxonomy matters operationally. A rate_limit_exceeded response means slow down: the worker applies jittered exponential backoff and retries per item, never per run. An insufficient_quota means stop: free-tier quota is gone until the 08:00 UTC reset, so hammering it wastes attempts and dead-letters rows. Both kinds have landed in dead_letter — six 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED rows sat there alongside four legitimate empty-caption skips, awaiting a human’s triage.

Where data goes stale or corrupt

Failures are loud. Drift is quiet. Three quiet zones are worth pinning on a strip.

The pattern across all four: nothing crashes. Gold simply stops being true — a stale prompt hash, an un-uploaded media file, a null engagement column, a roster row that never became a bronze file. The seams that make the architecture clean (pure transforms, external worker) are the same seams where staleness hides, because no single run owns the whole path.

Why it is this way

The two-world split was not an accident. It was two deliberate decisions, each defensible on its own terms.

ADR-0002 (decided 2026-07) moved enrichment out of Dagster runs entirely. The reasoning: enrichment calls a rate-limited LLM for hours at a time, absorbing 429s and waiting out the 08:00 UTC quota reset, and must survive crashes and Dagster restarts. The obvious alternative — Dagster Pipes, a Dagster-launched subprocess — was rejected because a run blocks until its subprocess exits. Correct for a short dbt job; wrong for hours-long rate-limited work holding a run slot open. So enrichment became a standalone worker, and gold_analyses became an externally-materialized AssetSpec whose sole writer is the worker, reporting via REST.

ADR-0003 (decided 2026-08) set the determinism boundary: no LLM or API calls anywhere in the Dagster transform graph. A transform is deterministic, idempotent, cheap to replay; an external call is stochastic, rate-limited, and paid. The media-cache work set the precedent — bytes download at ingestion, never inside a transform — and the ADR generalized it: network I/O lives at exactly two boundaries, ingestion and the worker. Silver onward is hermetic. The DAG enqueues candidates and consumes gold; it never blocks on Gemini.

Both decisions hold up. The pain was never the fences — it was that the gate through the second fence was left unattended. ADR-0002 chose an external worker but said nothing about who keeps the consume loop alive; the one-shot CLI was the simplest answer, and issue #24 is the bill for that simplicity.

The machinery at hand

The five-minute primer got you to assets and “what makes an asset run.” The three options below are built out of Dagster machinery the primer deliberately skipped, because until now nothing in datalake used it. All of it is the same idea wearing different clothes: Dagster wants the decision “when does this run?” to live in code that Dagster keeps evaluating, not in a person’s shell. We already have schedules and assets; the fixes need three more-native pieces — sensors (stateful polling that can replace a human re-run), external-state triggers (so “a profile was added” is a first-class event), and asset-centric automation conditions (freshness-driven re-runs inside the graph).

Sensors, and the cursor that makes them cheap. A sensor is a function Dagster calls repeatedly — on an interval, an event, or a run-status change. It inspects some state and either requests a run or explains why not. Its power for our case is the cursor: a small blob Dagster persists between evaluations. A tick reads the cursor, looks at external state, decides, and writes a new cursor. That is what makes a sensor “stateful polling” rather than a busy loop — each tick is cheap and resumable, and it survives restarts. This is the primitive that turns issue #24’s manual “re-run the worker” into something that happens on its own.

Three kinds of trigger, one idea. Dagster’s “what fires a run” vocabulary is broader than a schedule. A basic sensor polls an external condition on an interval (above: the queue, or a remote job’s status). An asset sensor fires when a specific asset is materialized — so “labels landed, go enrich” can be a trigger, not a schedule. A run-status sensor fires on a run succeeding or failing — the hook for “that harvest run finished, do the next thing.” And a Source / external trigger is the same shape pointed at state Dagster does not own: making “a profile was added to the roster” readable as an event is what Option B leans on. The recent Dagster line has been folding the older “SourceAsset” vocabulary into external assets (AssetSpec) plus the sensor that reacts to them — which is exactly the shape we already have for gold_analyses.

Asset-centric automation conditions. Since Dagster’s declarative-automation work, the recommended way to express “run when” for assets that live inside one code location is an AutomationCondition on the asset itself — eager() (run when upstream updates), on_cron() (on a schedule after upstream is ready), on_missing() (materialize missing work), and freshness as a first-class idea. The system evaluates conditions about every 30 s and launches runs when they hold. Sensors remain the right tool for triggering work outside the graph — an external worker, an async batch — which is why Option A (sensor-driven worker) and Option C (in-graph gated enrichment, automation-condition-driven) are two sides of Dagster’s own “choose your automation” guidance rather than competing inventions.

Two more that the options assume. Resources carry an API client and its config (the two-flavor 429 taxonomy, model/tier) into assets or sensors, so the client’s retry and backoff knowledge lives in one testable place. Asset checks attach invariants — freshness, and our check_prompt_currency — to a materialization. And incremental materialization lets an asset rewrite only the rows that changed — for enrichment, the column-nullability gate (only posts missing signal) is the key to Option C. Each option below names the exact pieces it leans on.

The shape of the fix

This remains an open investigation, not a decided plan. The research note frames the option space; the direction is consistent across all of them — keep the determinism boundary, close the orchestration vacuum. Each option below names the exact Dagster pieces it leans on (the machinery above), grounds itself in a pattern that is already working in the wild, and is honest about where it sits relative to Dagster’s own guidance. The criterion the five whys settled is the same for all three: the pipeline should not need a person to advance, and nothing should look “stuck” when the only missing ingredient is a poller.

Option A · sensor-driven external worker

Keep the external worker (ADR-0002 intact) and make the consume/harvest loop a Dagster sensor citizen: sensors with cursors poll for queue work and for terminal gemini-batch jobs, and request the worker or harvest run when either is true. The direct cure for #24, and the smallest move.

Option A in detail — submit, then a sensor harvests

The mechanism. Two loop-shaped responsibilities, each a sensor with its own cursor. The consume sensor looks for claimable batch_items and, when the worker is idle, requests a worker run. The harvest side handles async work: when a run submits a gemini-batch job, it persists the returned job_id (status pending) in ops.sqlite and returns — the run ends immediately rather than blocking for hours. A harvest sensor then polls those pending job ids on an interval; when a remote job is terminal, it requests the harvest run that fetches results and writes gold_analyses. This is exactly the stateful-polling shape in Figure 7.

Grounding. This is the canonical Dagster answer to “external work that takes hours,” and it is not exotic: a widely-shared write-up on orchestrating bulk embedding/LLM API work lays out precisely this as its primary pattern — bulk API — submit + sensor poll, where the run submits, persists the job id, ends, and a lightweight sensor (running on a minimum_interval_seconds of minutes) polls the remote job and triggers the downstream harvest by RunRequest. Its stated benefits map one-to-one onto our needs: no Dagster worker sits open for the batch duration, the job id is persisted so polling survives restarts, and the sensor tick stays cheap and within its timeout. It is also the pattern Dagster’s own docs describe for reacting to external systems, and Dagster’s “choose your automation” guidance puts a basic sensor exactly here: poll an external condition, emit a run request. ADR-0002’s worker and AssetSpec stay as they are; we add the citizen that keeps them moving.

Where it is challenged. Two subtleties. First, a sensor evaluation must not itself run the long work — it requests a run (the worker), which is the idiomatic boundary; a sensor that tried to call Gemini inline would block its tick and defeat the point. Second, “consume” and “harvest” are two different external states (a queue with work vs. a remote job finishing), so they are cleanly two sensors with two cursors, not one overloaded loop. The one-shot worker CLI and the --mode gemini-batch path both survive; only the human who re-runs them is automated away.

Option B · profiles as first-class sources

Treat each tracked profile as a first-class external trigger so that adding a profile to the roster is an ingestion trigger (issue #23), instead of a URL list a human feeds a manual run. The 50 accounts from issue #22 would have entered the lake automatically.

Option B in detail — a roster change becomes an event

The mechanism. The roster — profiles in ops.sqlite — is state Dagster does not own, so “a new profile appeared” needs a bridge into the graph. The canonical Dagster shape is a basic sensor with a cursor that reads the roster, compares it against the cursor’s last-seen high-water mark, and requests a scrape run for any profile that is new or not yet scraped — the same stateful-polling machinery as Figure 7, pointed at a table instead of a queue. (The older “SourceAsset” framing of this — track an external object and react to its changes — has been folded by Dagster into external assets plus the sensor that watches them, so this is squarely on the supported path.) Scrape config moves from a hand-fed URL list to per-profile rows that the sensor turns into work.

Grounding. Polling a database table for newly-added rows and launching work per change is one of Dagster’s most documented sensor idioms — the file-watching sensor in the docs is the same skeleton, and “new rows since the cursor” is a textbook cursor use case (it is precisely the reason cursors exist: keep the poll cheap and idempotent). The 50 accounts in issue #22 that were added but never scraped are the concrete payoff: each becomes a trigger, not a forgotten line in a config list.

Where it is challenged. The issue itself flags the open question: per-profile fan-out (a scrape run per account) multiplies complexity and run overhead for no benefit when an Apify actor scrapes many accounts at once — and Apify reliability (login walls) already makes per-actor runs fragile. The pragmatic middle is a sensor that detects changes to the roster but still requests one consolidated scrape over the affected profiles, rather than one run per profile. So Option B is really a decision about granularity of the trigger, not about whether the roster drives ingestion — it should; the question is how finely.

Option C · enrichment in the asset graph

Bring enrichment into the asset group as an explicitly-gated enrichment asset behind an incremental (column-nullability) gate — the job-search-toolkit shape. Cost: relaxes ADR-0003’s hermetic-transform rule, acceptable only if the asset is explicitly the enrichment seam and the work is bounded.

Option C in detail — enrichment as a gated in-graph asset

The mechanism. Where Options A and B keep the API call outside the graph, C lets an explicit enrichment asset call Gemini in-process, behind an incremental gate: its query reads only rows that still need signal — a column-nullability test (signal IS NULL) or a stale prompt_hash — so a re-run costs work only for the rows that are actually missing it, not a full replay. Enrichment is scoped per-dimension (per profile, not per post) so the same profile is not re-enriched every run. Because the asset lives in the graph, lineage, freshness, asset checks (check_prompt_currency), and Dagster-native backfills all apply for free, and its automation is the asset-centric kind: a freshness or eager-style condition re-runs it when upstream updates or signal goes stale.

Grounding. This is the “no bulk API — cached, threaded enrichment” shape: a single asset fans work across a bounded ThreadPoolExecutor/async executor, memoizes against a content-hash cache so only changed inputs reach the API, and upserts idempotently. Dagster’s own guidance supports bounded in-process fan-out for inference and enrichment — its “when sync isn’t enough” async-executor material is aimed squarely at exactly this workload — and it matches the repo’s job-search-toolkit precedent. The incrementality is the load-bearing part: without a nullability gate this is just the old full-replay antipattern in a nicer coat.

Where it is challenged. This is the option that relaxes ADR-0003: a stochastic, paid, rate-limited call now lives inside an asset, so the graph is no longer hermetic by construction. That is defensible only if (a) the asset is unambiguously the enrichment seam — named and tagged as the one place the graph may touch the API — and (b) the work is bounded: minutes, not the hours-long batches that are Option A’s reason to exist. The community decision rule is duration-driven: single-asset in-process polling is fine when work is minutes; hours/days of external work is when you move to sensor-based polling. In our terms: bounded per-profile enrichment can be Option C; hours-long gemini-batch harvesting should stay Option A’s sensor.

These are not mutually exclusive, and the investigation has not decided among them. Option A and Option B are complementary — a roster-triggered scrape feeding a sensor-harvested enrichment keeps every API call outside the graph; Option C trades that guarantee for Dagster-native lineage and is better reserved for the bounded work. What the options share is the thing #24 proved was missing: a poller that lives in Dagster, not in a person’s terminal.
